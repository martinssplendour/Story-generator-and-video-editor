One day, I’ll tell you how pretending to be broke helped me discover that some men are more interested in family trees than relationships.

Actually, that day is today.

I grew up in a wealthy Nigerian family. Not “comfortable” wealthy. I mean the kind where people become unusually polite after hearing your surname.

My father’s success opened many doors for me. It also made trusting people complicated. I wanted a man who loved me, not my father’s contacts, my family name, or whatever he imagined was inside my bank account.

Unfortunately, the men I met were talented actors.

Kola spent three months telling me money meant nothing to him. Then he asked if my father could support his logistics company: one motorcycle and an Instagram page. Apparently, believing in his dreams required my father to buy three trucks.

Then there was Femi. He cooked, sent thoughtful messages and insisted on paying for our first dates. I finally told him about my family. Two weeks later, he arrived at my house wearing a suit. I thought he was surprising me with dinner. No. He had brought my father a business proposal. Thirty-seven slides. That relationship ended somewhere between “market opportunity” and “expected return on investment.”

After that, I became suspicious of everybody. “Tell me about yourself” sounded like “Please list your family’s assets.” I stopped wearing expensive jewellery and arrived at dates in an ordinary car. I once said I worked as my father’s assistant. The man lost interest immediately. At least he was efficient.

My friend Amaka said I was behaving like the heroine in a low-budget spy film. Then she introduced me to Splendoure.

I created a simple plan: a film at the cinema, then dinner at a small restaurant nearby. Nothing extravagant. Just a movie, good food and, hopefully, a normal conversation.

That was how I met David.

He arrived seventeen minutes late because he entered the wrong cinema hall and watched ten minutes of a children’s cartoon before realising something was wrong. I laughed so hard I forgot to interrogate him.

During the film, David whispered predictions about the plot. Every single one was wrong. He confidently announced that the detective was the villain. The detective died three minutes later.

At dinner, we argued about the ending and talked until the staff began cleaning around us. David never asked about my surname, car or father. He asked about my book and remembered I hated raisins. For the first time in years, I enjoyed someone without wondering what he wanted.

We kept seeing each other. But the closer we became, the more frightened I felt. David was falling for a carefully edited version of me.

Three months later, he invited me to his sister’s engagement dinner at a hotel. I didn’t know my father was attending a charity event in the same building.

Then someone called my full name.

My father crossed the lobby with two politicians and enough security to destroy every secret I had. He hugged me and said, “So this is the young man you’ve been hiding from us?”

David looked at him. Then at me. Then at the security team.

After dinner, he asked to speak outside. I prepared myself for the questions, the sudden business idea, or the breakup.

Instead he said, “So your father is that man? This explains why you looked personally offended when I suggested we share popcorn.”

I told him everything. David listened, then said, “I understand why you were afraid. But you have to let me decide who I am. You can’t punish me for auditions I never attended.”

For several days, his replies became shorter. I thought I had finally met someone genuine and ruined it by expecting him to become like everybody else.

Then a notification appeared. David had created another plan on Splendoure: “Cinema Rematch—because someone still owes me popcorn.” One spot available.

I joined.

That evening, I told him the whole truth: the money, the fear and how lonely it felt to wonder if anyone could love me without wanting something.

David held my hand and said, “I don’t need anything from your father.” Then he looked at our tiny dinner and added, “Although if he knows the owner, please ask why six prawns are being called a meal.”

We’re still together. He refuses my father’s help, buys the cheapest popcorn even when I’m paying, and still predicts every film incorrectly.

I spent years hiding who I was because I wanted somebody to love the real me. I didn’t find him by pretending to be poor or testing every man I met. I found him after creating one simple cinema-and-dinner plan on Splendoure.

The plan helped us connect. Honesty gave us a reason to stay connected.

And David eventually confessed that he recognised my surname on our first date. He simply thought it was a coincidence.

So perhaps our relationship survived because the man I chose is handsome, kind—and occasionally very slow.
